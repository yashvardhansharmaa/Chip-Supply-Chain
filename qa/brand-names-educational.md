# Names for an educational one-stop resource

Options only. Nothing else in the repo changed. Checks already settled in `qa/brand-names.md` are not repeated.

## What the field actually calls this subject

The decisive finding: one institution uses two vocabularies, split by purpose.

CSET names the **object** plainly and the **policy** aggressively. Saif Khan's flagship is "The Semiconductor Supply Chain: Assessing National Competitiveness" ([cset.georgetown.edu](https://cset.georgetown.edu/publication/the-semiconductor-supply-chain/)), and "chokepoint" is not in the title (note that the brief's remembered subtitle, "Assessing National Security Implications", is not the published one). The word appears when a document tells a government what to do: "Preserving the Chokepoints: Reducing the Risks of Offshoring Among U.S. Semiconductor Manufacturing Equipment Firms" ([cset.georgetown.edu](https://cset.georgetown.edu/publication/preserving-the-chokepoints/)), and "Securing Semiconductor Supply Chains", which "recommends applying export controls on chokepoints" ([cset.georgetown.edu](https://cset.georgetown.edu/publication/securing-semiconductor-supply-chains/)).

Then CSET built a teaching tool and dropped the word. Its Emerging Technology Observatory calls it the **Supply Chain Explorer**, "a guide to the vast, complex, and critical supply chain for advanced computer chips", "designed to quickly orient non-experts" ([eto.tech](https://eto.tech/tool-docs/chipexplorer/)). Guide, explorer, orient. No chokepoint.

Everyone else, when teaching, does the same. SIA and BCG: "Strengthening the Global Semiconductor Supply Chain in an Uncertain Era". SIA's schoolroom is **Semiconductors 101**, with the page "How are Semiconductors Made?" ([semiconductors.org](https://www.semiconductors.org/semiconductors-101/how-are-semiconductors-made/)). SEMI teaches "From Wafer to Chip: The Basics of Semiconductor Manufacturing" ([semi.org](https://www.semi.org/en/event/wafer-chip-basics-semiconductor-manufacturing-americaseu)). The Commerce review sat inside "Building Resilient Supply Chains". The EU statute is a framework "for strengthening Europe's semiconductor ecosystem"; the UK has a **National Semiconductor Strategy**, METI a Semiconductor and Digital Industry Strategy. Compute governance says "supply chain" too: GovAI's "Computing Power and the Governance of Artificial Intelligence" argues compute is governable because it is "produced via an extremely concentrated supply chain" ([governance.ai](https://www.governance.ai/research-paper/computing-power-and-the-governance-of-artificial-intelligence)).

Popular explainers never use the word. ASML's education pages are "All about microchips", "How microchips are made", "Get the facts about microchips" ([asml.com](https://www.asml.com/en/technology/all-about-microchips)), with no "supply chain" and no "chokepoint". Asianometry runs a Semiconductor "Course". Chris Miller is the exception that proves it: "Chip War: The Fight for the World's Most Critical Technology" sells on conflict, and is narrative, not reference.

**What a newcomer types.** Google autocomplete, checked today. "how are ai chips" completes to *made, different, different from gpus, designed*. "semiconductor supply" completes to *chain, diagram, companies, map*. "what is lithography" completes to *in semiconductor manufacturing, in chip making, asml*. And "compute choke" returns an empty list, `["compute choke",[]]`, while "chip choke" returns choker necklaces and choke coils. Nobody arrives by that door.

**Security-framed words:** chokepoint, leverage, weaponised interdependence, bottleneck, dependence, denial, control. **Neutral words:** supply chain, how it is made, stage, primer, explainer, guide, field guide, handbook, reference, index, explorer, inside.

## The fifteen

**Plain-descriptive**

1. **How AI Chips Are Made.** Student and journalist land on it from search; a staffer files it as background, not analysis. Promises education flatly. Citable, but reads like a video title. No abbreviation. Prior art: a freeCodeCamp article of the name, no site.
2. **Inside the Chip Supply Chain.** Holds the phrase people search, and "inside" promises the walk the site is. All three readers hear neutral reporting. Survives a footnote. Long in a URL, no initials. No prior art as a title.
3. **The Chip Supply Chain Explained.** Clearest promise here and the weakest title: "explained" reads as SEO filler and dates badly.
4. **Who Makes AI Chips.** Warm and searchable, but it answers half the site, since the stages matter as much as the firms. A staffer would expect a list, not seventeen chapters.

**Reference-shaped**

5. **The Chipmaking Field Guide.** Promises education, portability and completeness, which the index, the 220 suppliers and the 35 chips deliver. Footnotes cleanly. Prior art: SPIE publishes a Field Guide series in optics, so the form is established and the chipmaking slot is empty.
6. **The Semiconductor Primer.** Authoritative to a staffer, dry to a student. Crowded: "semiconductor primer" autocompletes to *Oppenheimer semiconductor primer* and *semiconductor primer substack*, an existing sell-side genre.
7. **The Compute Reference.** The most citable name here and the coldest. "Compute" signals the AI-governance reader; a journalist would not quote it. No prior art found.
8. **The Chipmaking Handbook.** Neutral, slightly textbook. "Handbook" implies procedure, and the site explains rather than instructs. Prior art is heavy in adjacent print (Microchip Fabrication, the Essential Guide to Semiconductors).

**One-word or coined.** This family fails, and the prior art is the reason.

9. **Chipwise.** Dead. chipwise.tech is a live semiconductor reverse-engineering firm, and both domains are registered.
10. **Fabline.** Reads like equipment, not education; a student would not guess what it is. fabline.com has been held since 2001 and serves nothing.
11. **Waferwork.** Dead. Wafer Works Corporation is a Taiwanese wafer maker with six factories, at waferworks.com.
12. **The Chipworks.** Factory-like, both domains free, but Chipworks was the Ottawa reverse-engineering firm TechInsights bought in 2016, which this audience remembers.

**Names that keep the structure**

13. **From Sand to Rack.** The whole arc in four words, memorable, unused (the cliché is "sand to silicon"). Reads as documentary, which costs a little authority. Cites well.
14. **Fifteen Stages.** True to the site, meaningless in a search box, and it breaks the day a sixteenth stage matters.
15. **Stage by Stage.** Already the site's meta description. Too generic to own, and stagebystage.com is taken.

## Titles for the top five

1. **How AI Chips Are Made.** Hero "Fifteen stages, one chip" / "From polished sand to the rack that runs it." Masthead "Every stage, and who controls it". Browser `How AI Chips Are Made`. The board becomes what a reader asks after learning how a stage works, and the ratings answer "could anyone else do this".
2. **Inside the Chip Supply Chain.** Hero "How AI chips are made" / "Fifteen stages, 220 suppliers, and who controls each one." Masthead "How AI chips are made, stage by stage". Browser `Inside the Chip Supply Chain · How AI chips are made`. "Inside" covers the walk and the controls board without either having to lead.
3. **The Chipmaking Field Guide.** Hero "How AI chips are made" / "A field guide to fifteen stages and 220 suppliers." Masthead "The AI chip supply chain, stage by stage". Browser `The Chipmaking Field Guide`. A field guide is expected to carry ratings and tables, so the board and the Extreme and High bands read as apparatus, not as argument.
4. **Compute Chokepoints** (kept). Hero "How AI chips are made" / "Every stage of the supply chain, and who controls it." Masthead "How AI chips are made". Browser `Compute Chokepoints · How AI chips are made`. The board and the ratings are most at home here, which is the problem: they stop looking like two features among six.
5. **From Sand to Rack.** Hero "How AI chips are made" / "Fifteen stages from polished sand to the finished rack." Masthead "The AI chip supply chain". Browser `From Sand to Rack · How AI chips are made`. The board sits at the end of the journey, as the rules on where the rack may go.

## Recommendation

Lead with **Inside the Chip Supply Chain**, or **How AI Chips Are Made** if reach matters more than the footnote. It holds the exact string people type, survives a citation, and promises a walk through a thing rather than a verdict on it. It also leaves "chokepoint" free for the job it is good at, naming the card on each chapter. Keep the chokepoint cards. Retire the chokepoint masthead.

**The plain answer.** No. "Compute Chokepoints" does not stop the educational reader, and that is not the fear worth having. Policy readers like the word; none will be put off. The real cost is that the name never reaches the student or the enthusiast at all, because it describes neither the object nor the question. It says what the author concluded, not what the site contains, and autocomplete shows the door it opens onto is empty.

So a subtitle is not enough, though not because the tone needs fixing. A subtitle rescues the promise for someone already on the page. It cannot rescue a title tag, a search result, a shared link or a bookmark, which is where a name does its only real work. The change is cheap, because the descriptive line already exists in three places: the print title, the meta description and the masthead all say some form of "How AI chips are made" or "The AI chip supply chain". Swapping which line is the name and which is the tagline is most of the rename.
