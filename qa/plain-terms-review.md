# "In plain terms" review, pass 3

Rubric, applied as a policymaker with no technical background: (a) opens with what the stage does, in one plain sentence; (b) says who does it and how few they are; (c) says why it matters for supply or policy; (d) every acronym spelled out or avoided; (e) at most one analogy, and only if it clarifies; (f) 60 to 110 words; (g) no hedging clutter.

## 01 Introduction and Goals

Scored before: (a) fail, the paragraph opened on the car analogy and never said plainly what happens; (b) partial, "one or two credible suppliers" but no actor anywhere and every verb passive; (c) pass; (d) pass; (e) pass, one analogy; (f) pass, 96 words; (g) pass. Rewritten for (a) and (b).

Before:

> Building an AI chip, the kind that trains and runs an artificial intelligence model, is like building a car in which the engine, the gearbox and the wheels each come from a single factory somewhere in the world, with no second factory able to make them. About a dozen separately manufactured objects, each from a different country, are stacked into one finished package. Most of those objects have one or two credible suppliers, and replacing any of them takes years rather than months. That is why a rule written in one capital city can slow computing everywhere.

After (99 words):

> An AI chip, the kind that trains and runs artificial intelligence models, is assembled rather than made in one place. A packaging line stacks about a dozen separately manufactured objects, each from a different country, into one finished package. It is like building a car whose engine, gearbox and wheels each come from a single factory, with no second factory anywhere able to make them. Most of those objects have one or two credible suppliers, and replacing any of them takes years rather than months. That is why a rule written in one capital city can slow computing everywhere.

The statement now comes first and the analogy second, so a reader who does not need the car can skip it. "A packaging line stacks" replaces "are stacked into", which had no actor.

What a policymaker would still not understand: which country or firm holds which of the dozen objects, so the paragraph tells them a chokepoint exists without telling them where; and what "a packaging line" is, though the phrase carries its own meaning well enough to pass.

## 02 Chip Design, EDA and IP

Scored before: (a) fail, opened on the analogy; (b) pass but anonymous, "three companies" unnamed; (c) pass; (d) pass; (e) borderline, the city analogy plus "printing plates" as a second figure for the mask set; (f) pass, 94 words; (g) pass. Rewritten for (a), (b) and (e).

Before:

> Designing a chip is like drawing the street plan of a city of billions of buildings, where every road has to be walkable in less than a billionth of a second. No one draws it by hand. The layout is generated, checked and signed off by software sold by three companies, and a foundry will only accept a design that has passed their particular tools. Once the plan goes to the factory, changing one street costs a new set of printing plates and several months. Withhold the software and the design cannot be manufactured.

After (107 words):

> Chip design turns an idea for a processor into a complete blueprint, with every one of billions of transistors placed and wired. It is like drawing the street plan of a city that size, where every road has to be walkable in less than a billionth of a second. No one draws it by hand. Software from three companies, Synopsys, Cadence and Siemens, generates the layout, checks it and signs it off, and a chip factory accepts only designs that have passed those tools. Changing one street afterwards costs a fresh set of printing stencils and several months. Withhold the software and the design cannot be made.

The three companies are now named, which is the single most useful thing this paragraph can give a regulator. "Foundry" became "chip factory", and the passive "is generated, checked and signed off" became an active clause with the software as its subject.

What a policymaker would still not understand: why only three companies, since the lock-in comes from foundry certification, which the paragraph has no room to explain; and that "printing stencils" are the photomasks the rest of the site calls masks.

## 03 Silicon and Wafers

Scored before: (a) fail, "Sand is melted and then pulled slowly out of the melt" is a passive process description that never says what the stage produces or what it is for; (b) pass; (c) partial, the policy stake was implied by "none of them is American or Chinese" but never stated; (d) pass; (e) pass, one analogy; (f) pass, 99 words; (g) pass. Rewritten for (a) and (c).

Before:

> Sand is melted and then pulled slowly out of the melt like a candle being dipped, except what comes out is a single crystal, one cylinder in which every atom sits on the same repeating grid. That cylinder is sliced into discs 300 millimetres across and polished until nothing on the surface stands more than a few atoms proud. The silicon has to be pure to about one foreign atom in a hundred billion, and the flatness has to hold right to the edge. Only five firms can do it reliably, and none of them is American or Chinese.

After (108 words):

> Every chip starts as a mirror-polished disc of silicon 300 millimetres across. A furnace melts purified sand, and a machine pulls a cylinder out of the melt the way a candle is dipped, except that what comes out is a single crystal, every atom on the same repeating grid. Saws slice that cylinder into discs, polished until nothing on the surface stands more than a few atoms proud. The silicon has to be pure to about one foreign atom in a hundred billion, and the flatness has to hold right to the edge. Only five firms can do it reliably, and none of them is American or Chinese.

The stake now leads: every chip starts here. The furnace, the machine and the saws are named as the actors that were missing.

What a policymaker would still not understand: which five firms, and that "purified sand" stands in for polysilicon, an industry of its own that the body then introduces; nor why the disc is 300 millimetres and not some other size.

## 04 Chemicals, Gases and Photoresist

Scored before: (a) marginal, the first sentence names the material and its job but the second was passive with no actor; (b) pass, "nine tenths of the world's photoresist comes from Japan"; (c) pass; (d) pass; (e) pass, one analogy plus one scale image; (f) pass, 99 words; (g) pass. Edited rather than rewritten, for (a).

Before:

> Photoresist is photographic film for chips. A wafer is coated with a film thinner than a soap bubble, light shines through a stencil, and the exposed parts dissolve away, leaving a pattern the next machine can etch. A few hundred other gases and liquids deposit, strip and clean around it. All of it must be clean to about one stray metal atom in a trillion, and each formula is approved for one factory and one product at a time. That is why nine tenths of the world's photoresist comes from Japan, and a second source takes years, not weeks.

After (103 words):

> Photoresist is photographic film for chips. A coating machine spreads a film thinner than a soap bubble across the wafer, light shines through a stencil, and the exposed parts dissolve away, leaving a pattern the next machine can etch. A few hundred other gases and liquids deposit, strip and clean around it. All of it has to be clean to about one stray metal atom in a trillion, and each formula is approved for one factory and one product at a time. That is why nine tenths of the world's photoresist comes from Japan, and a second source takes years rather than weeks.

The opening analogy is the best one on the site and was left alone. The only structural change is the actor in sentence two.

What a policymaker would still not understand: what "etch" then does to the wafer; why Japan rather than anywhere else, which the body answers with qualification lock-in rather than with any secret; and that the chapter's other half, the gases, is barely visible here. Neon and nitrogen trifluoride, which carry the chapter's two real supply shocks, do not appear in the paragraph at all, and there is no room for them inside 110 words. Anyone reading only the plain box will think this stage is photoresist alone.

## 05 Lithography

Scored before: (a) fail, the paragraph opened on the analogy fragment "Lithography is photographic printing, shrunk", which tells the reader what lithography resembles before telling them what it does; (b) pass, "Only one company has ever built such a machine ... a single Dutch firm"; (c) partial, "which is why a single Dutch firm is a chokepoint" names the concentration but not what follows from it; (d) pass, no acronyms; (e) pass, one analogy; (f) pass, 94 words; (g) pass. Two mechanical faults as well: "A stencil ... is held up to a lamp" is passive with no actor, and "four times smaller than the stencil" dangles off "film" rather than off the image. Rewritten for (a), (c) and the actor.

Before (94 words):

> Lithography is photographic printing, shrunk. A stencil of one layer of the circuit is held up to a lamp, and the machine projects that image onto a silicon wafer coated in a light-sensitive film, four times smaller than the stencil. Finer circuits need shorter-wavelength light, and the shortest light now in use is absorbed by air and by glass, so the whole machine runs in a vacuum and every lens has to be a mirror. Only one company has ever built such a machine, which is why a single Dutch firm is a chokepoint.

After (100 words):

> Lithography prints the pattern of a circuit onto the chip. It is photography, shrunk: the machine holds a stencil of one layer up to a lamp and projects it, four times smaller, onto a silicon wafer coated in a light-sensitive film. Finer circuits need shorter wavelengths, and the shortest light now in use is absorbed by air and by glass, so the machine runs in a vacuum and every lens has to be a mirror. Only one company has ever built such a machine, and it is Dutch, which is why export controls on advanced chipmaking run through one firm.

The first sentence now states the job; the analogy follows as a colon rather than replacing the statement, so the printing image survives. The machine is the subject of the second sentence, and "four times smaller" attaches to the image it describes. The last clause names the policy consequence, which is what a regulator actually needs from this box: not that ASML is a chokepoint in the abstract, but that the control regime is built on it.

What a policymaker would still not understand: why nobody else has built one, which the chapter answers only in "The chokepoint" (Zeiss optics, Trumpf lasers, two decades); what a wafer is, since the word arrives undefined; and the fact that the same firm also sells the older deep ultraviolet machines that are the actual subject of the Dutch licensing rules, which the paragraph cannot fit and which is where most export-control argument sits.

## 06 Photomasks and Pellicles

Scored before: (a) pass, "A photomask is the printing plate for one layer of a chip" is the cleanest opener of the four; (b) pass, "Two Japanese firms"; (c) partial, "which is why a small and obscure business sits under every leading-edge chip" describes the situation rather than the risk; (d) pass; (e) pass, one analogy, and it is the right one; (f) pass, 92 words; (g) pass. One word was also wrong for this site: "one plate stamps that layer millions of times" uses the verb the lithography chapter reserves for Canon's nanoimprint tool, which stamps rather than projects. Edited for (c) and that verb.

Before (92 words):

> A photomask is the printing plate for one layer of a chip. The machine shrinks its picture four times onto the wafer, so one plate stamps that layer millions of times. No glass is transparent to the short light now in use, so the plate is not a window but a mirror of about forty pairs of ultrathin layers, and any speck of dust on it repeats on every chip. Two Japanese firms make almost all of those mirrors, which is why a small and obscure business sits under every leading-edge chip.

After (106 words):

> A photomask is the printing plate for one layer of a chip. The machine shrinks its picture four times onto the wafer and prints that layer over and over, so one plate shapes millions of chips. No glass is transparent to the short light now in use, so the plate is not a window but a mirror of about forty pairs of ultrathin layers, and any speck of dust on it repeats on every wafer it prints. Two Japanese firms make almost all of those mirrors, which is why an obscure business almost nobody has heard of can hold up every leading-edge chip in the world.

"Stamps that layer millions of times" became "prints that layer over and over, so one plate shapes millions of chips", which keeps the scale and removes the collision with nanoimprint. "Repeats on every chip" became "repeats on every wafer it prints", because the point is that the defect is systematic across a whole run, not that one chip is spoiled. The closing clause now says what the concentration can do, which is the sentence a regulator would quote.

What a policymaker would still not understand: that the two Japanese firms make the blank plate rather than the finished mask, and that the finished mask is usually made by the chip factory itself, a distinction the body then draws and this paragraph flattens; and that the pellicle, half the chapter's title and its one unsolved problem, is not mentioned here at all, because there is no room for a second object inside 110 words.

## 07 Deposition and Etch

Scored before: (a) fail, "If lithography is the printing, this is the building" only parses for a reader who has just read chapter 05, and the plain box is also shown on its own on the Briefing page; (b) pass, "Four firms in America, Japan and the Netherlands"; (c) pass, "China has come closer to matching them here than anywhere else" is exactly the policy point; (d) pass; (e) fail, two analogies in two sentences, the printing-versus-building contrast and the layer cake, and the second one then fights the verb "burn"; (f) pass, 102 words; (g) pass. Rewritten for (a) and (e).

Before (102 words):

> If lithography is the printing, this is the building. A chip is built like a layer cake: lay down a film a few atoms thick, print a pattern on it, burn away what the pattern does not protect, and repeat more than a thousand times. The film has to reach the bottom of a hole hundreds of times deeper than it is wide, and the burn has to eat one material and leave the one beside it. Four firms in America, Japan and the Netherlands make most of these machines, and China has come closer to matching them here than anywhere else.

After (100 words):

> Deposition and etch build the chip itself, like a layer cake: lay down a film a few atoms thick, print a pattern on it, burn away everything the pattern does not protect, then do it again, more than a thousand times over. The film has to reach the bottom of a hole hundreds of times deeper than it is wide, and the burn has to eat one material and leave the one beside it untouched. Four firms in America, Japan and the Netherlands make most of these machines, and China has come closer to matching them here than anywhere else.

The first sentence now names the stage and its job, and the layer cake, the better of the two analogies, is the only one left. "Leave the one beside it" gained "untouched", which is the whole difficulty of selectivity and was missing.

What a policymaker would still not understand: which four firms, since naming them would cost more words than the paragraph has; that "burn away" is chemistry rather than heat; and the reason China's progress here matters, which is that these tools are bought by the thousand rather than the dozen, so the failure of a control here looks nothing like the failure of a control on lithography.

## 08 Metrology and Inspection

Scored before: (a) partial, "Every chip factory is really a quality-control operation" is a claim about factories rather than about this stage, and it takes a second sentence to say what the machines do; (b) pass, "One American firm sells about seven times as many of those machines as its nearest rival"; (c) fail, no supply or policy consequence anywhere in the paragraph, only a yield fact; (d) pass; (e) pass, no analogy needed; (f) pass, 97 words; (g) marginal, "is really a" is filler. Rewritten for (a), (c) and (g).

Before (97 words):

> Every chip factory is really a quality-control operation. Machines print and build a pattern a thousand times over, and after many of those steps another machine looks at the wafer: are the lines the right width, did this layer land on the last one, has a speck of dirt killed a circuit. On a die as big as an AI accelerator, halving the number of stray specks lifts the share of working chips from about half to about seven in ten. One American firm sells about seven times as many of those machines as its nearest rival.

After (109 words):

> This stage is the factory's quality control. Machines build a chip in a thousand steps, and after many of them another machine inspects the wafer: are the lines the right width, did this layer land on the last one, has a speck of dirt killed a circuit. On a die as big as an AI accelerator, halving the stray specks lifts the share of working chips from about half to about seven in ten. One American firm sells seven times as many of those machines as its nearest rival, so a fab cut off from it can buy every other tool and still not learn why its chips fail.

The paragraph now ends on the consequence rather than on a market share, and the consequence is the chapter's own argument, moved up from "The chokepoint": a sanctioned fab can obtain scanners and etchers and still fail, because without sensitive inspection it cannot find out why its yield is low. That is the one thing in this chapter a regulator needs and could not have guessed.

What a policymaker would still not understand: what "quality control" involves at this scale, that is, that the machines measure rather than reject, and that their output is a map of where the process went wrong rather than a pass or fail; why the same firm dominates every family at once, which the body attributes to optics, electron columns and decades of catalogued defects; and the yield numbers themselves, which are the chapter's most quotable claim and, as printed in the body, the only pair without a citation attached.

## 09 Transistors and the Front End

Scored before: (a) fail, the paragraph opened on the valve analogy and never said that this stage is where the switches get built; (b) partial, "three companies" counted but named nobody; (c) partial, "which is why a regulator can name them" never says name them in what; (d) pass; (e) pass, one analogy; (f) pass, 91 words; (g) pass. Rewritten for (a), (b) and (c).

Before:

> A transistor is a valve on a pipe. Current runs along a silicon channel, and a voltage on the gate above it opens or shuts the flow. Make the channel short enough and the valve stops sealing: current leaks through even when the gate says off, so a chip burns power doing nothing. The fix is to wrap the gate around all four sides of the channel, which takes about a thousand process steps in exact order. Three companies can run that sequence, which is why a regulator can name them.

After (106 words):

> The front end of a chip factory builds the switches. Each one is a valve on a pipe: current runs along a silicon channel, and a voltage on the gate above it opens or shuts the flow. Make the channel short enough and the valve stops sealing, so current leaks through even when the gate is off and the chip burns power doing nothing. The fix is to wrap the gate around all four sides of the channel, which takes about a thousand steps in exact order. Only TSMC, Intel and Samsung can run that sequence, which is why an export rule can name them all.

The stage now has a name and a job in the first six words, and the analogy is subordinated to it. The three firms are named, which is the most useful thing this paragraph can hand a regulator, and the closing clause says what naming them is for. "The gate says off" became "the gate is off"; "process steps" became "steps", since the paragraph has already said what is happening.

What a policymaker would still not understand: why wrapping the gate takes a thousand steps rather than ten, which is the whole substitution argument and needs the body; and that "the front end" has a back end, defined one paragraph earlier in the chapter lead but not here.

## 10 Foundries and Fabs

Scored before: (a) pass; (b) fail, "the world shares a few of them" gives the fewness and no name, and "a few of them" has an unclear referent; (c) partial, "most of the newest ones sit in Taiwan" states the concentration but not why it matters; (d) pass; (e) borderline, the print shop plus the operating theatre; (f) pass, 94 words; (g) partial, "What makes it hard is that" is an inflating opener. Rewritten for (b), (c) and (g).

Before:

> A foundry is a print shop for chips. Other companies send in designs, and the foundry runs them through the same building on the same tools. What makes it hard is that the building costs tens of billions of dollars, the air inside has to be cleaner than an operating theatre, and a wafer crosses hundreds of machines over several months without ever touching that air. Nobody sells enough chips to fill a shop like that alone, so the world shares a few of them, and most of the newest ones sit in Taiwan.

After (105 words):

> A foundry is a print shop for chips: other companies send in designs, and the foundry runs them through the same building on the same tools. The building costs tens of billions of dollars, the air inside is cleaner than an operating theatre, and a wafer crosses hundreds of machines over several months without ever touching that air. No design house sells enough of one product to fill a shop like that, so the world shares a handful of them. TSMC runs the biggest, and all six of its largest plants sit in Taiwan, which is why one island's politics reaches the whole chip supply.

"What makes it hard is that" is gone and the three facts stand on their own. "Nobody sells enough chips" became "No design house sells enough of one product", which is the actual economics and names the actor. TSMC appears, the six plants are counted, and the last clause states the policy stake instead of leaving the reader to infer it.

I kept two figures of speech rather than one. The print shop is the analogy; the operating theatre is a scale comparison, and the editing brief asks for exactly that kind of anchor. Recorded here as a deliberate reading of criterion (e) rather than an oversight.

What a policymaker would still not understand: what a wafer is, which the chapter's third paragraph assumes; that "the newest" plants and "the biggest" are the same six; and why nobody has built a seventh outside Taiwan, which is the chapter's closing argument.

## 11 Memory and HBM

Scored before: (a) fail, the paragraph opened on a cook analogy whose payoff, "the answer is a wider door", mixes two images and explains nothing; (b) pass, three firms named; (c) fail, no reason a regulator should care appears anywhere; (d) pass, high bandwidth memory spelled out; (e) marginal, one analogy that does not clarify; (f) pass, 95 words; (g) pass. Rewritten for (a), (c) and (e).

Before:

> A cook can chop faster than anyone can fetch the ingredients, so the answer is a wider door. High bandwidth memory stacks up to sixteen memory chips into a cube, drills copper wires straight down through them, and sits the cube a few millimetres from the processor. Stacking is the hard part: each chip is ground thinner than a human hair, the whole cube must stay under a fixed height so it does not foul the cooling plate, and one bad layer ruins all of them. After (107 words):

> High bandwidth memory is where an AI chip keeps the numbers it is working on, and it is built to hand them over fast. It stacks up to sixteen memory chips into a cube, drills copper wires straight down through them, and sits the cube a few millimetres from the processor. Stacking is the hard part: each chip is ground thinner than a human hair, the cube must stay under a fixed height or it fouls the cooling plate, and one bad layer ruins the rest. The cook is gone. What a policymaker would still not understand: why a wider connection has to mean a stack rather than a bigger chip, which the paragraph after it answers with the pin count; and what "a single line in an export rule" contains, which is the bandwidth-density threshold given later in full.

## 13 Substrates and PCBs

## 14 Test and Assembly

## 15 Systems and Networking

Scored before: (a) fail, it opened with a general principle about clusters and only said what the stage does in sentence two; (b) fail, it said nothing about who does the work or how few they are; (c) partial, "the tightest supply in this layer is a laser material" implies the stake without naming it; (d) pass; (e) pass, no analogy; (f) pass, 87 words; (g) pass. Before:

> A cluster runs only as fast as its slowest link, so this stage is built to keep every accelerator in step. After (91 words):

The job leads; the slowest-link principle now explains why the links go optical instead of standing in for the job. After (107 words):

> A data center's job is to feed and cool the machines inside it. The scale has changed: a hair dryer pours about two kilowatts of heat out of a hand-sized nozzle, and one rack of AI chips draws seventy of them in a box the size of a wardrobe, every watt of it turning into heat that has to be carried away in water rather than air. The building is the easy part. Getting the electricity to the door is the hard one, because the transformers, switchgear and turbines that connect a site to the grid come from a few suppliers and are ordered years in advance.

The hair dryer survives, demoted from opener to evidence, and "domestic" went with it as a word doing no work. "Come from a few suppliers" is the missing (b), and is what makes the lead times a chokepoint rather than a queue.
