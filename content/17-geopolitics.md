# Geopolitics

The United States is still changing its export controls, and China is still building its response to them.

## The shape of the contest

An export control is a rule that requires the government's permission for a sale. The government names an item and a buyer, and from then on its own companies must hold a license before they ship that item to that buyer. The American government extends the rule to goods made abroad with American technology. Few governments can use such a rule to much effect, because it works only when their own firms sell something the buyer cannot get elsewhere. The machines, software and chemicals used to make an advanced chip come from a short list of firms in the United States, the Netherlands, Japan and South Korea. China can copy the simpler machines within a few years. It cannot yet copy the machine that prints the finest circuits, or the chemicals that machine needs. It has also yet to show it can make high-bandwidth memory, the stacked memory beside an AI chip, in volume.

In September 2022 National Security Advisor Jake Sullivan called securing "as large a lead as possible" in technologies such as AI a national security imperative ([source](https://www.csis.org/analysis/where-chips-fall-us-export-controls-under-biden-administration-2022-2024)). The October rules that followed were the first to apply his doctrine of a small yard with a high fence, meaning strict controls on a narrow set of technologies ([source](https://www.csis.org/analysis/where-chips-fall-us-export-controls-under-biden-administration-2022-2024)). Those rules went beyond the old aim of staying two technology nodes ahead of China and tried to push China's capability back below the level it had already reached ([source](https://www.csis.org/analysis/choking-chinas-access-future-ai)).

Whether a sale needed a license once depended on who was buying and why. The 2022 rules also made it depend on where the item was going ([source](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2022/10/13/2022-21658/implementation-of-additional-export-controls-certain-advanced-computing-and-semiconductor)). They required a license for any advanced AI chip bound for China, and for tools bound for Chinese fabs beyond set technology thresholds ([source](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2022/10/13/2022-21658/implementation-of-additional-export-controls-certain-advanced-computing-and-semiconductor)). Most applications were presumed denied ([source](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2022/10/13/2022-21658/implementation-of-additional-export-controls-certain-advanced-computing-and-semiconductor)). But equipment for the China fabs of firms headquartered in the United States or in allied and partner countries was reviewed case by case ([source](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2022/10/13/2022-21658/implementation-of-additional-export-controls-certain-advanced-computing-and-semiconductor)).

The United States leads in chip design and in three kinds of tools, the Netherlands in lithography and Japan in materials. No country is self-sufficient, so the United States and China can each use export controls to hurt the other.

## The US export-control campaign, 2018 to today

- **17 August 2020, the Foreign Direct Product Rule.** In May 2019 the Commerce Department added Huawei and 68 affiliates to the Entity List, its roster of buyers that need a license ([source](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2019/05/21/2019-10616/addition-of-entities-to-the-entity-list)). When Huawei bought foreign chips instead, Commerce extended the license requirement to any foreign item made with US technology and bound for Huawei ([source](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2020/08/20/2020-18213/addition-of-huawei-non-us-affiliates-to-the-entity-list-the-removal-of-temporary-general-license-and)).
- **7 October 2022, the first country-wide rules.** They capped the performance of exportable AI chips and set technology thresholds for advanced fabs ([source](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2022/10/13/2022-21658/implementation-of-additional-export-controls-certain-advanced-computing-and-semiconductor)).
- **17 October 2023, the density fix.** Nvidia had designed its China-specific chips to stay under the 2022 limit on how fast a chip passes data to other chips ([source](https://cset.georgetown.edu/article/bis-2023-update-explainer/)). So Commerce's Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) dropped that limit ([source](https://cset.georgetown.edu/article/bis-2023-update-explainer/)). Since then BIS has identified controlled chips by total processing performance, a measure of computing power, by performance density, and by whether they are designed or marketed for data centers ([source](https://cset.georgetown.edu/article/bis-2023-update-explainer/)).
- **2 December 2024, the widest rule.** The rules issued that day covered 24 equipment types and three software tools, added the first controls on high-bandwidth memory, and put 140 entities on the Entity List ([source](https://www.bis.gov/press-release/commerce-strengthens-export-controls-restrict-chinas-capability-produce-advanced-semiconductors-military)).
- **15 January 2025, AI Diffusion.** The rule required a license to ship the most powerful AI chips and computers to any destination worldwide, then reopened access through exceptions, none of which covered China ([source](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/01/15/2025-00636/framework-for-artificial-intelligence-diffusion)). On 13 May 2025 BIS said it would not enforce the rule ([source](https://www.bis.gov/press-release/department-commerce-announces-rescission-biden-era-artificial-intelligence-diffusion-rule-strengthens)). It said it would issue a replacement rule ([source](https://www.bis.gov/press-release/department-commerce-announces-rescission-biden-era-artificial-intelligence-diffusion-rule-strengthens)).

On 9 April 2025 the government told Nvidia it needed a license to ship its H20 chip to China and to countries under a US arms embargo ([source](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581025000082/nvda-20250409.htm)). It granted H20 licenses in August 2025 ([source](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581025000209/nvda-20250727.htm)). Nvidia said US officials expected the government to receive 15 percent of the revenue from licensed H20 sales, though no regulation codifies that requirement ([source](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581025000209/nvda-20250727.htm)). Under a rule in force since 15 January 2026, BIS reviews exports of Nvidia's H200 and AMD's MI325X to China or Macau case by case ([source](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/01/15/2026-00789/revision-to-license-review-policy-for-advanced-computing-commodities)). The rule caps those exports at 50 percent of the total processing performance of the same chips shipped to US customers ([source](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/01/15/2026-00789/revision-to-license-review-policy-for-advanced-computing-commodities)).

## Ways around the controls

Renting out time on a chip does not count as exporting it, so a Singapore data center selling time on an H200 already installed there moves nothing across a border ([source](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-15/subtitle-B/chapter-VII/subchapter-C/part-734/section-734.13)). The AI Diffusion rule said that API access to a model, and rented capacity for inference, "are not prohibited" ([source](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/01/15/2025-00636/framework-for-artificial-intelligence-diffusion)).

The American rules reach that trade through the firms holding the chips. A January 2026 license for China or Macau requires the applicant to obtain the recipient's know-your-customer procedures ([source](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-01-15/html/2026-00789.htm)). It must also obtain the recipient's list of customers renting computing power in China and seven other places, among them Russia and Iran ([source](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-01-15/html/2026-00789.htm)). The license also depends on a promise that no algorithm trained on the chips is served to blacklisted or military end users ([source](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-01-15/html/2026-00789.htm)).

The United States has approved large AI chip sales to the United Arab Emirates and Saudi Arabia. In November 2025 Commerce authorized two AI companies, G42 of the Emirates and HUMAIN of Saudi Arabia, to buy up to 35,000 Nvidia GB300s each under security and reporting conditions ([source](https://www.commerce.gov/news/press-releases/2025/11/statement-uae-and-saudi-chip-exports)). On 10 July 2026 Commerce moved the Emirates into the export-control group that holds close US allies ([source](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/07/14/2026-14132/enhanced-favorable-treatment-for-the-united-arab-emirates-under-the-export-administration)). It named the Emirati government, G42 and US-headquartered AI companies as recipients that need no license for controlled AI chips and systems ([source](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/07/14/2026-14132/enhanced-favorable-treatment-for-the-united-arab-emirates-under-the-export-administration)).

Smugglers route chips past the controls through Southeast Asia. Since 14 July 2025 Malaysia has required a permit to export, transship or transit any high-performance US-origin AI chip ([source](https://www.miti.gov.my/miti/resources/Media%20Release/%5BFINAL%5D_MITI_Press_Stmt_Malaysia_Regulates_Trade_of_US_AI_Chips_2025-07-14.pdf)). On 25 March 2026 the Justice Department announced charges against three people accused of using Thai companies' names to order servers with export-controlled chips for China ([source](https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/chinese-national-and-two-us-citizens-charged-conspiring-smuggle-artificial-intelligence)). The orders included one for $170 million that was never completed ([source](https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/chinese-national-and-two-us-citizens-charged-conspiring-smuggle-artificial-intelligence)).

The House select committee on China cites a median estimate of 140,000 chips smuggled to China in 2024 ([source](https://chinaselectcommittee.house.gov/media/press-releases/protecting-us-tech-china-committee-and-bipartisan-bicameral-leaders-unite-to-stop-ccp-ai-chip-smuggling)). A bill in Congress, the Chip Security Act, would require location verification on controlled chips within 180 days of enactment ([source](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/1705/text)). The House Foreign Affairs Committee passed it 42-0 on 26 March 2026 ([source](https://huizenga.house.gov/news/documentsingle.aspx?DocumentID=404259)). It has not become law.

## Allies: the Netherlands, Japan, Korea and Europe

US tool controls alone would only shift the sales to Dutch and Japanese suppliers, so the campaign depends on those two governments. Both have aligned their own controls on chipmaking equipment with the American rules ([source](https://www.csis.org/analysis/where-chips-fall-us-export-controls-under-biden-administration-2022-2024)).

- **The Netherlands.** The Dutch government began licensing advanced deep-ultraviolet tools on 1 September 2023 and widened the controls in September 2024 ([source](https://www.government.nl/latest/news/2024/09/06/the-netherlands-expands-export-control-measure-advanced-semiconductor-manufacturing-equipment)). On 1 April 2025 it extended them to measurement and inspection tools ([source](https://www.government.nl/latest/news/2025/01/15/klever-export-controls-on-advanced-semiconductor-manufacturing-equipment-to-be-tightened)).
- **Japan.** Japan's controls on advanced manufacturing equipment took effect on 23 July 2023, apply to all destinations and name no country ([source](https://www.csis.org/analysis/csis-translation-updated-japanese-export-controls-high-performance-semiconductor)).
- **South Korea.** BIS revoked the Validated End-User status that let Samsung's and SK hynix's China subsidiaries and Intel Semiconductor (Dalian) receive US equipment without individual licenses, effective 31 December 2025 ([source](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/09/02/2025-16735/revocation-of-validated-end-user-authorizations-in-the-peoples-republic-of-china)). BIS said it would license those fabs to keep running, but not to expand capacity or upgrade technology ([source](https://www.bis.gov/press-release/department-commerce-closes-export-controls-loophole-foreign-owned-semiconductor-fabs-china)). SK hynix, which bought the Dalian fab from Intel, received an annual BIS license in December 2025 to ship approved US equipment to its China fabs during 2026 ([source](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2120882/000119312526299963/d32785d424b4.htm)).

A BIS rule effective 29 September 2025 extended Entity List restrictions to any company at least 50 percent owned by one or more listed firms ([source](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/09/30/2025-19001/expansion-of-end-user-controls-to-cover-affiliates-of-certain-listed-entities)). On 30 September the Dutch economy minister invoked the Goods Availability Act against Nexperia, a Nijmegen chipmaker that supplies Europe's car industry ([source](https://www.government.nl/latest/news/2025/10/12/minister-of-economic-affairs-invokes-goods-availability-act)). He cited governance failures that risked moving technology out of Europe ([source](https://www.government.nl/latest/news/2025/10/12/minister-of-economic-affairs-invokes-goods-availability-act)).

Both were suspended in November. BIS suspended its rule for a year on 10 November ([source](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/11/12/2025-19846/one-year-suspension-of-expansion-of-end-user-controls-for-affiliates-of-certain-listed-entities)). The minister suspended his order on 19 November ([source](https://www.government.nl/documents/2025/11/19/update-on-invoking-goods-availability-act)). Europe holds a chokepoint in leading-edge lithography, but none in the mature-node chips its car industry uses.

The MATCH Act, a bill introduced on 8 April 2026, would ban sales of immersion deep-ultraviolet scanners to China ([source](https://www.foreign.senate.gov/press/rep/release/risch-ricketts-kim-introduce-match-act-level-the-global-playing-field-for-us-tech)). It would also name five Chinese companies, CXMT, Hua Hong, Huawei, SMIC and YMTC, in statute ([source](https://www.foreign.senate.gov/press/rep/release/risch-ricketts-kim-introduce-match-act-level-the-global-playing-field-for-us-tech)). It would give allies 150 days to match those limits before the United States extends the Foreign Direct Product Rule on its own ([source](https://www.foreign.senate.gov/press/rep/release/risch-ricketts-kim-introduce-match-act-level-the-global-playing-field-for-us-tech)).

## China's response

In 2024 China's Ministry of Industry and Information Technology reportedly told Chinese telecom operators to strip foreign chips out of their networks by 2027 ([source](https://www.csis.org/analysis/chinas-localization-drive-semiconductors-gains-impetus-allied-chip-export-controls)).

China has also retaliated against American suppliers. Micron, the American memory maker, failed a review by the Cyberspace Administration of China (CAC) on 21 May 2023 and lost its critical-infrastructure buyers in China ([source](https://www.csis.org/analysis/micron-aggression-right-response-beijings-ban-us-chipmaker)). In September 2025 the CAC told firms including ByteDance and Alibaba to cancel Nvidia orders ([source](https://cset.georgetown.edu/newsletter/september-18-2025/)). When the United States licensed H200 sales in February 2026, China restricted them, as it had restricted licensed H20 sales ([source](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581026000075/nvda-20260726.htm)). In the quarter ended 26 July 2026, Nvidia's H200 shipments under those licenses came to less than 1 percent of its data-center revenue ([source](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581026000075/nvda-20260726.htm)).

China's Ministry of Commerce (MOFCOM) has also restricted exports of minerals.

- **3 December 2024.** MOFCOM banned exports of gallium, germanium, antimony and superhard materials to the United States, one day after the American chip rules ([source](https://cset.georgetown.edu/publication/china-rare-earth-export-ban/)).
- **4 April 2025.** It required export licenses for seven medium and heavy rare earths ([source](https://english.mofcom.gov.cn/Policies/AnnouncementsOrders/art/2025/art_0dd87cbee7b045bf93fabe6ab2faceee.html)).
- **9 October 2025.** It extended licensing to foreign-made goods containing Chinese rare earths worth 0.1 percent or more of their value ([source](https://cset.georgetown.edu/publication/mofcom-notice-2025-61/)). MOFCOM suspended that rule on 7 November 2025 until 10 November 2026 ([source](https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/zwgk/zcfb/art/2025/art_b1ec77dd3f0d4762952904df7cdaadec.html)).
- **November 2025.** After Donald Trump and Xi Jinping met at the Asia-Pacific summit, MOFCOM suspended the gallium ban for a year ([source](https://www.csis.org/analysis/us-china-trade-truce-has-not-solved-gallium-problem)).

China repealed none of these controls, and the April licensing rules still apply.

Chinese toolmakers held 10 to 15 percent of their home market before 2022, 25 percent in 2024 and 35 percent in 2025 ([source](https://www.csis.org/analysis/chinas-localization-drive-semiconductors-gains-impetus-allied-chip-export-controls)). They hold about 40 percent of the Chinese market for etch and deposition tools ([source](https://www.csis.org/analysis/chinas-localization-drive-semiconductors-gains-impetus-allied-chip-export-controls)). But in September 2025 the Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS) judged that making its own extreme-ultraviolet machines remained firmly out of China's reach despite massive state investment ([source](https://www.csis.org/blogs/strategic-technologies-blog/breakthroughs-or-boasts-assessing-recent-chinese-lithography)).

Chinese chipmakers remain behind at the leading edge. SMIC's most advanced process, N+3, reaches the transistor density of TSMC's 6 nm-class process without extreme-ultraviolet scanners ([source](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/steel-smic-n3-teardown)). It prints its tightest layers in several passes on deep-ultraviolet tools ([source](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/steel-smic-n3-teardown)). In September 2025 the research firm SemiAnalysis expected Huawei to ship about 805,000 of its Ascend AI chips in 2025, with dies made by both TSMC and SMIC ([source](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/huawei-ascend-production-ramp)).

SemiAnalysis judged that memory would limit Huawei's output first. By its count, a stockpile of roughly 13 million memory stacks was enough for about 1.6 million Ascend 910C packages ([source](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/huawei-ascend-production-ramp)). It expected CXMT, China's own memory maker, to make only about 2 million stacks in 2026, enough for 250,000 to 300,000 more ([source](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/huawei-ascend-production-ramp)). Huawei said in September 2025 that its Ascend 950 chips, due in 2026, would carry high-bandwidth memory of its own design ([source](https://www.huawei.com/en/news/2025/9/hc-xu-keynote-speech)). It gave no figure for how much of that memory it would make ([source](https://www.huawei.com/en/news/2025/9/hc-xu-keynote-speech)).

## Industrial policy: CHIPS, Rapidus, Big Fund and the rest

The CHIPS and Science Act authorized about $52.7 billion ([source](https://www.csis.org/analysis/chinas-localization-drive-semiconductors-gains-impetus-allied-chip-export-controls)). By July 2025 Commerce had awarded $30.9 billion of direct funding to 19 companies for 40 projects, covering a median 14.2 percent of each project's capital cost ([source](https://files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-26-107882/index.html)). Commerce counted a federal tax credit, the Advanced Manufacturing Investment Credit, when it sized those grants ([source](https://files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-26-107882/index.html)). Since the One Big Beautiful Bill Act of 2025, the credit covers 35 percent of qualified investment in an advanced chip plant placed in service after 2025, up from 25 percent ([source](https://files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-26-107882/index.html)).

On 22 August 2025 the government converted $5.7 billion of unpaid Intel grants and $3.2 billion of Secure Enclave defense money into $8.9 billion of Intel stock, a 9.9 percent stake ([source](https://www.intc.com/news-events/press-releases/detail/1748/intel-and-trump-administration-reach-historic-agreement-to)).

The United States has also used tariffs to draw chip investment. The 2026 US-Taiwan trade agreement cuts the reciprocal tariff on Taiwanese goods to 15 percent in exchange for at least $250 billion of Taiwanese investment in US chip production ([source](https://www.cfr.org/articles/u-s-taiwan-trade-agreement-leaves-major-questions-open)). On 16 July 2026 TSMC announced another $100 billion for four more Arizona fabs at 2 nm or more advanced, bringing its total Arizona investment to $265 billion ([source](https://www.phoenix.gov/newsroom/ced-news/tsmc-announces-additional--100-billion-investment-in-arizona.html)).

The European Commission says the EU still depends on other countries for advanced chip manufacturing ([source](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/library/proposal-chips-act-20)). Its Chips Act 2.0 proposal, adopted on 3 June 2026, adds measures to support advanced chip production in the EU ([source](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/library/proposal-chips-act-20)).

Japan instead backs a homegrown chipmaker, Rapidus. It began prototyping 2 nm gate-all-around transistors at its IIM-1 plant in July 2025 and measured their first electrical characteristics ([source](https://www.rapidus.inc/en/news_topics/information/rapidus-achieves-significant-milestone-at-its-state-of-the-art-foundry-with-prototyping-of-leading-edge-2nm-gaa-transistors/)). In February 2026 it raised another 267.6 billion yen, about $1.7 billion, from a government agency and 32 companies, aiming for 2 nm mass production by 2027 ([source](https://www.rapidus.inc/en/news_topics/information/rapidus-secures-267-6-billion-yen-in-funding-from-japan-government-and-private-sector-companies/)). By June 2026 it had raised 424.95 billion yen in equity, the latest 150 billion yen from a state agency ([source](https://www.rapidus.inc/en/news_topics/information/20260605-2/)).

China spends more than the United States. The latest round of its National Integrated Circuit Industry Investment Fund, the Big Fund, reached $47 billion ([source](https://www.rand.org/pubs/perspectives/PEA4012-1.html)). CSIS puts cumulative state funding since 2014 at an estimated $150 billion and calls the CHIPS Act substantially smaller in scale ([source](https://www.csis.org/analysis/chinas-localization-drive-semiconductors-gains-impetus-allied-chip-export-controls)). The money has produced a domestic tool industry but no leading-edge node.

## Taiwan

No policy has reduced the world's reliance on Taiwan. Taiwan's foundries hold 78 percent of the world's contract chipmaking on the Taiwan Semiconductor Industry Association's 2025 count, and TSMC makes its 2 nm chips only in Taiwan ([source](https://www.stimson.org/2025/why-taiwan-fears-america-first-risks-eroding-its-silicon-shield/)). Taiwan held 69 percent of the world's logic capacity below 10 nm in 2022, by a 2024 count from the Semiconductor Industry Association and Boston Consulting Group ([source](https://www.semiconductors.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/Report_Emerging-Resilience-in-the-Semiconductor-Supply-Chain.pdf)).

Advanced packaging is in Taiwan too. Asked in October 2025 how much CoWoS packaging capacity he would add, TSMC's chairman said only that the company was "working very hard to narrow the gap between the demand and supply" ([source](https://investor.tsmc.com/english/encrypt/files/encrypt_file/reports/2025-10/6860312f04fd291d0f26b46c1234f84e6332717e/TSMC%203Q25%20Transcript.pdf)). He called wafer and packaging capacity both "very tight" ([source](https://investor.tsmc.com/english/encrypt/files/encrypt_file/reports/2025-10/6860312f04fd291d0f26b46c1234f84e6332717e/TSMC%203Q25%20Transcript.pdf)).

The silicon shield argument holds that this concentration deters an invasion, because destroying TSMC would cost an aggressor more than Taiwan is worth. The counterargument is that staying irreplaceable gives Taiwan's government reason to slow the US push to spread chipmaking beyond Taiwan.

Taiwan's rules keep TSMC's newest process at home first, so TSMC's Arizona fabs have run a generation behind its fabs in Taiwan ([source](https://www.stimson.org/2025/why-taiwan-fears-america-first-risks-eroding-its-silicon-shield/)). The Stimson Center expected 2 nm production in Arizona in 2028 at the earliest ([source](https://www.stimson.org/2025/why-taiwan-fears-america-first-risks-eroding-its-silicon-shield/)). TSMC said in October 2025 that it was "preparing to upgrade our technologies faster to N2 and more advanced process technologies in Arizona" ([source](https://investor.tsmc.com/english/encrypt/files/encrypt_file/reports/2025-10/6860312f04fd291d0f26b46c1234f84e6332717e/TSMC%203Q25%20Transcript.pdf)).

The Arizona fabs narrow the gap in wafer production, and TSMC has two advanced packaging fabs planned there ([source](https://investor.tsmc.com/english/encrypt/files/encrypt_file/reports/2025-10/6860312f04fd291d0f26b46c1234f84e6332717e/TSMC%203Q25%20Transcript.pdf)). But a fab without Taiwan's mask shops, chemical suppliers and trained crews is not a substitute. So the silicon shield still holds, though it is weakening.

## Who gains and who pays

- **Taiwan and Korea.** None of these policies will move leading-edge logic, memory stacking or advanced packaging out of Taiwan and Korea before 2030. Reshoring adds capacity elsewhere without taking any away from them.
- **The Netherlands and Japan.** Their toolmakers build the controlled machines, so the two governments gain a say in who gets them. China accounts for a falling share of the firms' sales. At Tokyo Electron, China's share fell from 44.4 percent in the year to March 2024 to 34.1 percent in the year to March 2026 ([source](https://www.tel.com/ir/library/report/pjuomj00000000tf-att/fy26q4transcript-e.pdf)). The company said the share dropped to 26.8 percent in the quarter to March 2026 because spending on leading-edge chips grew faster than spending on older ones ([source](https://www.tel.com/ir/library/report/pjuomj00000000tf-att/fy26q4transcript-e.pdf)).
- **The United States.** Its firms took 65 percent of the world's logic chip sales in 2022 ([source](https://chipexplorer.eto.tech/?parentNode=N0&selectedNode=N6)). Counting every type of chip, they took 53.4 percent of global sales in 2025, their highest share since 1984 ([source](https://www.semiconductors.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/SIA_2026_State-of-the-Semiconductor-Industry.pdf)). Commerce expects the CHIPS-funded projects to lift the US share of global leading-edge logic chip manufacturing from 0 percent in 2022 to 20 percent by 2030 ([source](https://files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-26-107882/index.html)).
- **China.** Mature-node chips, tools below the leading edge, DRAM and NAND are moving to Chinese suppliers. Extreme-ultraviolet machines, the photoresist they need and high-bandwidth memory in volume are not. In September 2025 SemiAnalysis expected China, without smuggled memory, to make fewer Ascend chips in 2026 than in 2025 ([source](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/huawei-ascend-production-ramp)).

The Gulf gains without making chips, because the July 2026 rule lets named Emirati buyers receive advanced computing items without a license ([source](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/07/14/2026-14132/enhanced-favorable-treatment-for-the-united-arab-emirates-under-the-export-administration)).

The US government also uses tariffs and an equity stake alongside licenses. Every licensed H200 bound for China pays a 25 percent tariff on its way through US inspection ([source](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581026000075/nvda-20260726.htm)).

## What to watch

- **November 2026.** Whether China renews the one-year suspension of its gallium ban or lets it expire ([source](https://www.csis.org/analysis/us-china-trade-truce-has-not-solved-gallium-problem)).
- **The MATCH Act.** Whether Congress passes the bill and allies match its limits on deep-ultraviolet tools ([source](https://www.foreign.senate.gov/press/rep/release/risch-ricketts-kim-introduce-match-act-level-the-global-playing-field-for-us-tech)).
- **CXMT's memory ramp.** In September 2025 SemiAnalysis judged that domestic memory would limit China's output of AI chips before logic wafers did ([source](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/huawei-ascend-production-ramp)).
- **SMIC's advanced-node ramp.** In September 2025 SemiAnalysis conservatively estimated SMIC's capacity at 7 nm and below at 45,000 wafers a month by the end of 2025, 60,000 in 2026 and 80,000 in 2027 ([source](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/huawei-ascend-production-ramp)). Yield, the share of chips on a wafer that work, is the open question.
- **Arizona's lag.** How far Arizona's newest process stays behind Taiwan's as TSMC's four new fabs there start production ([source](https://www.phoenix.gov/newsroom/ced-news/tsmc-announces-additional--100-billion-investment-in-arizona.html)).

## Takeaways

Japan and the Netherlands have aligned their own export controls with the American rules on chipmaking equipment ([source](https://www.csis.org/analysis/where-chips-fall-us-export-controls-under-biden-administration-2022-2024)). Chinese toolmakers supplied 35 percent of China's own chipmaking equipment market in 2025, up from 10 to 15 percent before 2022 ([source](https://www.csis.org/analysis/chinas-localization-drive-semiconductors-gains-impetus-allied-chip-export-controls)). When the United States licensed sales of Nvidia's H200 chip in February 2026, China restricted them ([source](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581026000075/nvda-20260726.htm)).

The most advanced chips come mainly from Taiwan. It held 69 percent of the world's capacity for logic chips below 10 nanometers in 2022, by the count of the Semiconductor Industry Association and Boston Consulting Group ([source](https://www.semiconductors.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/Report_Emerging-Resilience-in-the-Semiconductor-Supply-Chain.pdf)).
